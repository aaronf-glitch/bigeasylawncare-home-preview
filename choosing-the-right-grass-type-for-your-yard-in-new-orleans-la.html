Choosing the Right Grass Type for Your Yard in New Orleans, LA

August 20, 2026·8 min read·By the Big Easy Lawn Care team

Choosing a grass type in New Orleans comes down to sun, traffic, and how much maintenance you actually want to do. St. Augustine handles shade best and is the local default, Zoysia gives density and wear tolerance, Bermuda wants full sun and heavy use, and Centipede asks the least of you. Sun exposure decides the shortlist before any other factor gets a vote.

At Big Easy Lawn Care, we usually get the grass question after somebody has already bought sod. That is the expensive order to do it in, because the yard decides the grass rather than the other way around.

All four common choices here are warm-season grasses, so none of them stays green once frost arrives. What separates them is shade tolerance, how much traffic they take, and how much work they ask of you.

This guide walks through the realistic options and the questions that actually narrow them down. Contact us today to have someone look at your yard before you commit to a grass.

So What Actually Grows Here?

Four grasses cover the overwhelming majority of New Orleans lawns. Each one is genuinely good at something and genuinely bad at something else.

St. Augustine

St. Augustine is the default here, and for good reason: it tolerates more shade than any other warm-season grass commonly grown in the Gulf South. That matters enormously in a city full of mature live oaks.

The trade is texture and pest pressure. Blades are broad and coarse rather than fine, and chinch bugs treat St. Augustine as a preferred target, so it needs watching in hot, dry stretches.

It spreads by above-ground runners, which fills thin spots quickly but scalps easily on uneven ground. Most New Orleans yards with mature trees end up here by default.

Zoysia

Zoysia gives you a dense, fine-to-medium turf that feels good underfoot and crowds out weeds well once it fills in. It handles foot traffic better than St. Augustine and takes moderate shade.

The catch is patience. Zoysia establishes slowly compared to the alternatives, so you are buying a lawn that gets noticeably better in years two and three rather than one that looks finished immediately.

Because it fills in gradually, weeds get a window early on, and that first stretch needs more attention than homeowners expect. The payoff is density that holds.

Bermuda

Bermuda is the traffic champion. If your yard is a full-sun play area that takes constant use, nothing else recovers from wear as quickly.

It is also the least shade-tolerant of the four by a wide margin, and it spreads aggressively into beds and neighboring turf. Plan on edging as a permanent commitment rather than an occasional chore.

Its runners travel above and below ground, which is why it reappears in beds after you pull it. In an open, sunny yard with kids or dogs, that vigor is the point.

Centipede

Centipede is the least demanding grass on this list. It needs less feeding, grows slowly enough to reduce mowing frequency, and tolerates the acidic soils common in parts of the region.

The tradeoffs are color and toughness. It runs a lighter green that some homeowners dislike, and it does not take heavy traffic or bounce back from damage quickly.

It also dislikes heavy fertilizing, so the instinct to feed a struggling lawn tends to make it worse. Treated gently, it is the closest thing to a set-and-forget turf here.

Okay, How Do I Pick?

Four questions get you to an answer, and you can work through all of them in an afternoon. Take them in this order, because the first one eliminates the most options.

Start With Sun and Shade

Spend a day noting how many hours of direct sun each part of the yard actually gets. Not how sunny it feels, but hours of unobstructed sun on the grass itself.

Heavily shaded yards narrow the list fast, usually to St. Augustine and sometimes Zoysia. A full-sun yard keeps every option open, which is a nicer problem to have.

Light also shifts through the season, since deciduous trees leaf out while live oaks hold their canopy year round. Judge the yard when the trees are fully leafed.

Be Honest About Traffic

Count the real traffic, including kids, dogs, and the path everyone takes from the driveway to the back door. A lawn that gets crossed a hundred times a week needs a different grass than one that gets admired from the porch.

Bermuda and Zoysia handle wear best. Centipede handles it worst, and putting it on a play area is a decision you will regret by the second summer.

Traffic also concentrates rather than spreads, so the worn stripe by the gate matters more than the yard average. Fixing that one path is often easier than changing the lawn.

Decide How Much Maintenance You Want

This is the question people answer aspirationally and then regret. Be realistic about whether you will mow weekly in July, watch for pests, and keep up with feeding.

Centipede is the low-input choice. St. Augustine and Bermuda both want more attention, and Zoysia sits in between once established. Our lawn cutting service exists partly because people pick a grass that needs more than they have time for.

Appetite for upkeep changes with life, and the yard you can manage now may not be the one you can manage later. Choosing slightly below your ceiling is safer.

Look at What the Neighbors Have

Drive the block and note what is thriving on lots with similar sun exposure and similar tree cover. It is the cheapest soil-and-microclimate test available.

If a grass type is struggling everywhere on your street, that is real information. Local performance beats any general recommendation, including this one.

Ask, too, since most people will tell you what they planted and what it cost them in effort. That surfaces local pest and drainage problems no general guide mentions.

The Shade Thing (It Decides Most of It)

Shade is one of the most common reasons a New Orleans lawn fails, and it is the factor homeowners most often underestimate. A yard under filtered light for part of the day is a different growing environment than one sitting in open sun.

No warm-season grass thrives in deep shade. St. Augustine tolerates the most, but tolerance is not the same as thriving, and a spot under a dense live oak canopy may simply not support turf at all.

When that is the situation, the honest answer is often to stop fighting it. Ground cover, mulched beds, or a shade garden will look better and cost less than replacing failed sod every other year, which is a conversation our landscape restoration team has regularly.

What About Mixing Grass Types?

Homeowners ask this constantly, usually because one section of the yard keeps failing. It is a reasonable instinct and a difficult thing to execute well.

Warm-season grasses spread aggressively and compete rather than coexist, so a mixed lawn tends to become a patchwork with visible seams where two textures meet. Bermuda in particular will invade whatever is next to it.

Zoning is the better version of the idea. Rather than mixing species in one area, treat the shaded section as a different space entirely, with its own planting approach and its own edge.

Bottom Line

Sun exposure narrows your options, traffic and maintenance appetite pick the winner, and what is already thriving nearby confirms it. Getting that order right is the difference between a lawn that establishes and one you replace.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best grass for New Orleans lawns?

St. Augustine is among the most widely planted grasses in New Orleans because it tolerates more shade than any other warm-season option commonly grown in the Gulf South, which suits a city with heavy tree cover. Zoysia, Bermuda, and Centipede all outperform it on specific measures, so the best choice depends on your yard’s sun, traffic, and maintenance appetite.

What grass grows best in shade in Louisiana?

St. Augustine handles shade better than the other common warm-season grasses, and Zoysia manages moderate shade reasonably well. No warm-season grass thrives in deep shade, though. Under a dense canopy, ground cover or mulched beds usually outperform any attempt at turf and cost far less over time.

Which grass is best for heavy foot traffic?

Bermuda recovers from wear faster than any other grass commonly used here, which makes it the standard choice for full-sun play areas. Zoysia is the next best and offers a finer texture. Centipede handles traffic worst and should be avoided anywhere people and pets regularly cross.

What is the lowest maintenance grass in the Gulf South?

Centipede asks the least. It needs less feeding than the alternatives, grows slowly enough to reduce mowing frequency, and tolerates the acidic soils found in parts of the region. The tradeoffs are its lighter green color and its poor tolerance for traffic and damage.

Can you mix different grass types in one lawn?

It rarely works well. Warm-season grasses spread aggressively and compete rather than coexist, so mixed lawns tend to develop a patchy look with visible seams where textures meet. Zoning is the better approach: treat a problem area as its own space with its own planting plan and a defined edge.

Why does my grass keep dying under my tree?

Almost always insufficient light, compounded by the tree competing for water and nutrients at the root zone. Even shade-tolerant grass needs meaningful light to sustain itself, and dense canopy cover can drop below that threshold. Repeated sod replacement in the same spot usually signals the site cannot support turf.

Does St. Augustine grass get chinch bugs?

Chinch bugs favor St. Augustine over the other common warm-season grasses, and they tend to appear during hot, dry stretches. Damage often looks like drought stress at first, which is why it gets missed. Any patch that fails to respond to watering during hot weather is worth having inspected.

Is Zoysia worth it if it takes so long to establish?

It depends on your timeline. Zoysia produces a dense, fine-textured lawn that resists weeds and handles traffic well, but it fills in slowly and looks its best in later seasons rather than immediately. Homeowners who want a finished look right away are usually happier with a faster-establishing option.
